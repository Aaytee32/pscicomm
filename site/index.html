Two Ways a Cell Dies

Ischemic Stroke Cell-Death Sequelae

When blood flow stops, a neuron doesn't just "die"; it takes one of two routes, and which one depends on how severe the injury is.

In this video

This 2½-minute explainer follows the whole cell-death cascade of ischemic stroke on a single map: ATP collapse stalls the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ pour in and water follows into cytotoxic edema, depolarization opens NMDA receptors, and Ca²⁺ floods the cytosol. From there the cascade forks — severe injury fragments DNA and ends in necrosis; less severe injury runs the controlled programme.

Both apoptotic routes are drawn in full. Intrinsic: Ca²⁺ and oxidative stress dephosphorylate P-Bad; Bad neutralizes Bcl-2; Bax opens a pore in the outer mitochondrial membrane; cytochrome c escapes and assembles the apoptosome with Apaf-1, which switches on caspase 3 while Smac binds the IAPs and lifts the brake they hold on it. Extrinsic: a death ligand binds its receptor, FADD recruits pro-caspase 8, and caspase 8 cleaves caspase 3 directly. Both routes converge on caspase 3. HSP70 pushes back at four separate points.

For medical and pharmacy students.

A teaching schematic, not to scale, and not a quantitative model.

Stroke, Swallowing & Tube Feeding: 3 Things Families Need to Know

Aspiration, Pill Crushing & Medicare

Ama's stroke left her unable to swallow safely. This case-based explainer walks through three practical questions her care team and family faced.

In this video
  • Silent aspiration: food or liquid can enter the airway with no cough. Bedside checks can miss it; an X-ray swallow study can show it.
  • Crush or not? Extended-release tablets must never be crushed. Example: the metoprolol succinate ER label says "do not crush". Give one medicine at a time, flushed with water before, between and after.
  • Who pays? Under Original Medicare, home tube feeding is covered by Part B as a prosthetic device: 20% after the Part B deductible. "Permanent" means long and indefinite duration, not "can never improve".

For nursing, pharmacy and medical students, and for families navigating stroke care.

Sources: CMS LCD L38955 and Policy Article A58833 (Enteral Nutrition); Medicare.gov, Prosthetic devices; DailyMed metoprolol succinate ER label; ISMP "Do Not Crush" list; ASPEN Safe Practices for Enteral Nutrition (2017); AHA/ASA acute ischemic stroke guidelines (2019). Coverage rules as of November 2026; Medicare Advantage and supplemental plans differ.

Two Routes to the Cochlea: Types of Deafness Explained

Weber, Rinne & Cochlear Implants

Sound reaches the cochlea by two routes: through air and the ossicles, or through the bones of the skull. Every type of deafness, both tuning-fork tests, and the cochlear implant make sense once you know which route is broken.

In this video
  • Conduction deafness: a block in the outer or middle ear. Bone conduction still works, so it's never total.
  • Sensorineural deafness: damaged spiral-organ hair cells or cochlear nerve (e.g. acoustic neurinoma), so both routes fail
  • Weber test: fork on the mid-forehead; heard in the normal ear (nerve deafness) or louder in the affected ear (conduction deafness)
  • Rinne test: air vs bone; normally air > bone; bone > air in conduction deafness; both reduced in sensorineural loss
  • Cochlear implants: microphone → receiver under the skin → electrode array; each frequency activates its own electrodes, and patients relearn to hear

For medical and pharmacy students: neuroscience, ENT, clinical examination, and USMLE / NAPLEX / PLAB revision.

Source: Young PA, Young PH, Tolbert DL. Basic Clinical Neuroscience, 3rd ed. (Wolters Kluwer), pp. 165–168, paraphrased. Rinne results in hearing loss and the cochlea's frequency map are standard clinical teaching.

Two Roads to a Damaged Artery: How Cholesterol & Hypertension Drive Atherosclerosis

NOX2, Angiotensin II & ROS

In this real human artery, one side of the wall is more than five times thicker than the other: 0.25 mm here, 1.36 mm there. What builds it? Two roads that meet in the same damaged wall.

In this video
  • A real human coronary artery section, measured: the thickening and its fat-laden cells
  • Road 1: high cholesterol → ↑ NOX2 oxidase → reactive oxygen species
  • Road 2: hypertension → angiotensin II → AT1 receptor → ↑ NADPH oxidases
  • Where they meet: ↓ nitric oxide, sticky white cells and platelets, inflammation → endothelial dysfunction
  • The mouse evidence: block either road (NOX2 or AT1R knockout) and most of the damage is blunted

Atherosclerosis underlies both coronary and cerebral artery disease. For medical and pharmacy students: cardiovascular and stroke pathophysiology, and USMLE / NAPLEX / PLAB revision.

Source: Stroke: Pathophysiology, Diagnosis, and Management, 7th ed. (Grotta et al., Elsevier), Ch. 1 and Fig. 1.1, paraphrased and redrawn.

From Platelets to Plasmin: A Clot in 60 Seconds

Thrombin, Fibrin & t-PA

Inside a brain artery, a clot is built step by step, from the first platelets to the fibrin that holds it together, and the plasmin that can break it apart. Drawn in colored pencil.

In this video
  • Damaged lining: platelets stick first; in fast arterial flow, clots are platelet-rich
  • The platelet surface as a workbench: factors V and VIII speed thrombin generation
  • Thrombin cuts fibrinogen into fibrin, the clot's scaffold
  • Factor XIII, switched on by thrombin, cross-links the strands
  • t-PA from the lining turns plasminogen into plasmin on the clot surface, and fragments can travel downstream
  • Why fibrin content and cross-linking may affect how well clot-dissolving drugs work

For medical and pharmacy students: hematology, stroke, and USMLE / NAPLEX / PLAB revision.

Source: Stroke: Pathophysiology, Diagnosis, and Management, 7th ed. (Grotta et al., Elsevier), Ch. 2, "Thrombus formation", and Fig. 2.2, paraphrased and redrawn. A teaching schematic, not to scale.

Cerebral Autoregulation Explained: How the Brain Holds Its Blood Flow

CPP, CVR & Carotid Stenosis

Between 70 and 150 mmHg of perfusion pressure, cerebral blood flow barely changes. Here's how the brain does it, and when it fails.

In this video
  • The autoregulation plateau: flow falls ≤ 18%, then steeply outside 70–150 mmHg
  • The lever: arteriolar diameter, cerebrovascular resistance (CVR), a ~10 s lag, and the rise in CBV
  • Myogenic vs metabolic control: why local oxygen wins in vivo
  • Chronic hypertension shifts the curve: lower limit 100–120 mmHg, and why that matters for ischemia
  • Local CPP, not MAP: CPP = MAP − ICP
  • Finite capacity: autoregulation lost in hypercapnia, anemia, hypoxemia
  • Carotid stenosis: no effect until 60–70%, and the role of collaterals

For medical and pharmacy students: neurology, physiology, and USMLE / NAPLEX / PLAB revision.

Source: Stroke: Pathophysiology, Diagnosis, and Management, 7th ed. (Grotta et al., Elsevier), pp. 29–30, paraphrased. Curves are schematic.
